Once brands are grouped into worlds, a merchandiser wants to know which
brands sit between them: the entries a shopper passes through on the way
from one audience to another. Betweenness centrality counts how often a
brand lies on the shortest paths between other brands in the similarity
graph. Brands that connect many worlds are candidates for cross-world
navigation, or for exclusion from a collection that should stay inside one
world.

The statement builds the same Jaccard graph as the worlds query: 5,000
brands, edges where the shared-fan similarity reaches 0.02. Leiden assigns
the worlds and `cugraph_betweenness_centrality` scores the same edges. A
join of each brand's graph neighbors to their world labels counts how many
worlds the brand touches directly.

The connectors are mostly sellers and generic labels that the catalog
stores in its brand field. Department stores and retailers (Walmart,
JCPenney, Macy's, Claire's), house brands (Mainstays, Better Homes &
Gardens) and catch-all entries (As Seen on TV, Customized & Personalized,
Eyeshadow, US Vintage) hold most of the first fifteen positions, each with
neighbors in roughly 40 of the worlds. Samsung is the manufacturer with the
highest score; its 98 neighbors span 13 worlds. A merchandising team that
builds collections from the worlds should treat these entries as navigation
hubs, or remove them before the collection is assembled, and the catalog
team gains a list of brand entries that behave like stores.

Betweenness depends on the graph the statement defines. A different
similarity threshold changes which paths exist, and a brand with few
neighbors can score highly when it is the only link between two worlds. The
world counts use the Leiden labels of the same execution. Jaccard, Leiden
and betweenness run in one plan, and the similarity relation feeds both
graph calls and the neighbor join without leaving the GPU.
